# Selecting standalone clips from a call

## Your job

Given a recording or transcript of a call, find the **standalone clips** inside
it: self-contained videos, each one a single complete idea or a single problem
solved, postable as-is.

Your one job is **selection**: which spans of the call are worth becoming clips,
and exactly where each one starts and ends. You are not editing, styling, or
writing final titles. Get the selection right and the rest is easy; get it wrong
and nothing else helps. Spend your judgment here.

## What a clip is

One clip = **one complete thing**. One problem solved, or one idea taught, start
to finish. Someone who has seen nothing else from the call can watch it and get
full value.

Every clip is an arc:

- **Hook.** The setup: whose problem this is, or what idea is about to be taught,
  and why it matters.
- **Body.** The working-through.
- **Payoff.** The resolution: the answer, the recommendation, the point landed.

A span with all three is a clip. A span missing the payoff is not.

**The only criterion is value: every clip must be "too good to fail."** A clip
lives or dies purely on how much value it delivers, never on its length or
format. If a span is pure value end to end, it is a clip, whether that runs three
minutes or thirty. Do not select, cut, reject, or split a span to hit a time
window. Length is an output of where the value starts and stops, never an input.

## Step 1: Read the whole call and identify its shape

Before selecting anything, read the entire transcript and decide which kind of
call this is. This decides how you segment:

- **Q&A / hot-seat call.** Multiple people bring problems one at a time. Make one
  clip per person's problem.
- **Teaching / workshop call.** An expert teaches concepts, often with live
  examples. Make one clip per topic or principle.
- **Mixed.** A teaching call with extended hot-seats, or a Q&A with teaching
  detours. Use both: a clip per principle and a clip per substantial hot-seat.

Do not assume the format. A teaching call segmented as if it were Q&A, or the
reverse, produces wrong clips.

**Multi-part series:** if this recording is one session of a multi-part workshop,
process it on its own. Every clip must still stand alone; assume the viewer has
not seen the other parts. A concept introduced in an earlier part and applied in
a later one gets clipped wherever it is actually taught in full, not split across
parts.

## Step 2: Find candidate segments

Walk the whole transcript and mark every candidate clip span.

- **Q&A:** a segment starts when a new person is brought in or starts their
  question; it ends on the wrap-up of their problem ("thanks," "that makes
  sense").
- **Teaching:** a segment starts when a new topic or principle is introduced; it
  ends when that topic resolves and the next one begins.
- The speaker usually signposts boundaries out loud ("principle number two,"
  "next question," "let's talk about X"). These are your most reliable markers.

## Step 3: Triage for worthiness

This is the core of the job. Most of a call does NOT clip well. Keep a candidate
ONLY if all are true:

- [ ] It covers **one specific, nameable thing**: a real problem or a real idea,
      not chat, logistics, or rambling.
- [ ] It is **relatable**. Other people would recognize their own situation in it.
- [ ] It has a **clear payoff** a viewer can act on or take away.
- [ ] It **stands alone**, understandable with zero context from the rest of the
      call.
- [ ] No blocking privacy or confidentiality issue (see the note below).

Reject anything merely mildly interesting. Five strong clips beat twenty weak
ones, but do not under-select either: a call full of teaching should yield many
clips, not a handful. If a segment is genuinely strong, it is a clip, even if that
means the call produces fifteen of them. When truly unsure, reject.

**What filler looks like, and you reject it, never clip it:** going around the
room for introductions, scheduling and tech setup ("can you hear me," "unmute
yourself"), calling for the next person, bathroom or coffee breaks and the banter
around them, reading prep notes, recaps that only re-summarize what was already
taught, and closing thank-yous. Everything between the real content is filler;
knowing this sharply makes you more confident keeping everything that is not.

## Step 4: Set each clip's boundaries

For every segment that passed triage:

- **Start on the hook**, the first words of the setup. Cut dead air, cross-talk,
  "can you hear me," and the tail of the previous topic.
- **End on the payoff**, the last words of the resolution. Cut the wrap-up
  logistics and the transition into whatever comes next.
- **One topic per clip; never bundle.** If a span contains two distinct ideas, or
  two people's problems, it is two clips, not one. A clip must not promise one
  topic in its scope and then run fifteen minutes of others.
- **But never fragment a flowing piece either.** The opposite mistake is just as
  damaging: chopping one continuous idea into several clips to hit a length
  target. If a long teaching block flows as a single unbroken arc, one hook, one
  through-line, one payoff, it is ONE clip, however long it runs. The test is
  always the content, never the clock: two distinct ideas make two clips; one idea
  that flows into itself is one clip, even at thirty minutes.
- **Be precise.** Trim to the word, not the nearest minute. A clip that needs
  obvious hand-trimming afterward was not finished.
- **Do not force length.** Let the real start and end decide. A tight 3-minute
  principle and a 30-minute teaching block are both correct if that is where the
  idea begins and ends. Never pad a weak segment to reach a length, and never cut
  a flowing one short, or slice it up, to fit one.

## What you output

- For each clip: precise start and end timestamps, and one line on what it covers
  and why it passed triage.
- A short list of segments you rejected, one-line reason each, so a human can
  override.
- If the call contains a strong standalone testimonial about the expert, flag it
  separately with its timestamps; it can be prepended to a clip as social proof.

## A note on privacy and confidentiality

You surface candidates. The person publishing decides what is actually shareable.
Some spans are strong clips but should not be posted: a participant's sensitive
personal situation, anything that makes a named client look bad, material covered
under a confidentiality agreement, or content that exposes a specific business's
private numbers. Flag anything in this category so the human can make the call. Do
not invent a blanket rule that auto-rejects these; the judgment belongs to the
publisher, not the tool.

## What NOT to do

- Don't bundle multiple topics or problems into one long clip.
- Don't fragment one flowing idea into several clips to hit a window; if it flows
  into itself as a single idea, it is one clip, however long.
- Don't force clips toward a length target.
- Don't pad a weak segment to make it "long enough"; reject it instead.
- Don't under-select to look selective; every genuinely strong segment is a clip.
- Don't edit, add effects, or write final titles. That is a different job.
